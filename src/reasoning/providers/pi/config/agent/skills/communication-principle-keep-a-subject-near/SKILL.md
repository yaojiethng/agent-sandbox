# Keep a subject near

Give each subject one place, and keep all of its parts there.

**The pattern.**

| Instead of | Grouped by proximity |
|---|---|
| a caveat three sections from the rule it qualifies | the caveat under that rule |
| a definition wherever the term first appears | the definition beside its rules and caveats, where the reader first needs it |
| a caveat recorded only in a second document | the caveat beside the rule it qualifies |
| parts ordered as the author wrote them | parts ordered as the reader needs them: definition, rule, caveat |
| a section carrying a rule and a second, unrelated subject | one section per subject, with a pointer between them |

**Why.** Proximity is what a reader's working memory depends on. A reader who meets a rule without its caveat understands the rule too widely, and meets the caveat after that understanding has settled, so the correction costs a second pass over what they already read. A reader who comes for one subject and meets a second spends attention on text nobody asked for. The same order serves the next edit, because a writer who sees the whole subject at once changes every part and a writer who does not changes some.

Nothing reports either failure. Each half reads as complete, and so does each section.

**The four costs a writer checks.**

- **Working memory.** The reader holds the rule while reading the caveat.
- **Mental-model repair.** A rule read without its caveat has to be unlearned before the caveat lands.
- **Attention.** A section carrying a second subject spends attention nobody asked to spend.
- **The next edit.** A writer who cannot see the whole subject edits part of it.

**Where another rule wins.** A second copy is a different defect: the norm then has two sources of truth. `principle-single-source-of-truth` owns that defect, at `$AGENT_HOME/agent/skills/principle-single-source-of-truth/SKILL.md`, and the copy points at the source of truth.

This rule covers the other two cases: one source of truth whose parts the reader never reaches, and one place that carries a second subject.

**The test.** Read the document in the order it is written. At each caveat, ask whether the reader still holds the rule it qualifies. At each section, ask what one subject it serves. A caveat that reaches back further than one section means the parts are apart. A section that serves two subjects means one of them belongs elsewhere.
